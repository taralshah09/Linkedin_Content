# LinkedIn Post Drafts — Daily Bangers Reading List

Sequenced 1 post/day starting **2026-08-18**, following the order in `Daily bangers.md`. Uber H3 (already posted) is excluded. DDIA folded in from the Top 5 list since it wasn't covered elsewhere. Swap in real links before posting (marked `[link]`).

| Day | Date | Article | Post Content |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | 2026-08-18 | Time, Clocks, and the Ordering of Events — Leslie Lamport (1978) | Every distributed systems bug I've ever chased down eventually traces back to one question: what actually happened before what? Turns out that question has a name — Lamport's 1978 paper on logical clocks and the "happens-before" relation. No fancy hardware, no GPS, just causality reasoning on message passing. If you've ever debugged an out-of-order event stream, this is the paper explaining why your intuition about "time" was the bug all along ⏱️ [link] |
| 2 | 2026-08-19 | The Google File System — Google (2003) | Before I knew what a distributed filesystem even was, I was just appending logs to a single file and hoping it wouldn't grow too big. GFS is basically Google saying "what if failure was the default assumption, not the edge case." Chunkservers, a single master, append-heavy writes — it's the blueprint half of modern big data infra quietly still runs on 📁 [link] |
| 3 | 2026-08-20 | MapReduce: Simplified Data Processing on Large Clusters — Google (2004) | I wrote a dozen "group by and aggregate" LeetCode solutions before realizing that's literally what MapReduce industrialized across thousands of machines. Map, shuffle, reduce — an embarrassingly simple mental model that reshaped how an entire industry thinks about processing data it can't fit on one box 🗺️ [link] |
| 4 | 2026-08-21 | Bigtable: A Distributed Storage System for Structured Data — Google (2006) | Sparse, sorted, distributed, multi-dimensional map — that's Bigtable in one line, and it's the paper that basically invented the column-family store category. Pair it with GFS and you can watch Google's storage stack get built in real time, paper by paper 🧱 [link] |
| 5 | 2026-08-22 | Dynamo: Amazon's Highly Available Key-Value Store — Amazon (2007) | Dynamo is the paper where "always available, even if inconsistent" stopped being a compromise and became a design philosophy. Consistent hashing, vector clocks, quorum reads/writes — half of the NoSQL databases I've used are basically Dynamo cosplay 🛒 [link] |
| 6 | 2026-08-23 | Paxos Made Simple — Leslie Lamport | Consensus algorithms have a reputation for being unreadable, and honestly the original Paxos paper earned that. This one is Lamport's own "let me explain it better" attempt — and it actually works. If you want to understand how a cluster of machines agrees on literally anything, start here before Raft 🗳️ [link] |
| 7 | 2026-08-24 | Harvest, Yield, and Scalable Tolerant Systems — Fox & Brewer | CAP theorem gets quoted a lot and understood a little. This paper is the more useful, more nuanced version — harvest and yield as a spectrum instead of a binary pick-two. If a system design interview ever made you say "it depends," this is the paper that explains why that's the right answer 📊 [link] |
| 8 | 2026-08-25 | In Search of an Understandable Consensus Algorithm (Raft) — Ongaro & Ousterhout (2014) | Raft exists because Paxos was correct but nobody could teach it. Leader election, log replication, safety — explained so clearly that it's now the default consensus algorithm in half the systems you've used (etcd, Consul, CockroachDB). Understandability as a first-class design goal, wild concept 🧭 [link] |
| 9 | 2026-08-26 | Dapper — Google | Ever wonder how a single slow request gets traced across 40 microservices without anyone losing their mind? Dapper is the paper that answered that at Google scale, and every tracing tool you've used — Zipkin, Jaeger, OpenTelemetry — is downstream of it. Distributed tracing had to be invented once, this is that once 🔍 [link] |
| 10 | 2026-08-27 | The Log: What every software engineer should know about real-time data's unifying abstraction — Jay Kreps | This is the essay that made me stop thinking of a log as "just a file you write errors to." Kreps reframes the append-only log as THE core abstraction for data systems — databases, replication, stream processing, all the same idea wearing different clothes. Read this before you touch Kafka 📜 [link] |
| 11 | 2026-08-28 | Kafka: a Distributed Messaging System for Log Processing — LinkedIn | LinkedIn built Kafka because pub/sub systems of the time couldn't handle their event volume, and then it ate the entire industry's data pipeline. Read this right after Kreps' Log essay — it's the theory turning into the system 📨 [link] |
| 12 | 2026-08-29 | Spanner: Google's Globally-Distributed Database — Google (2012) | Spanner's whole trick is TrueTime — using atomic clocks and GPS to bound clock uncertainty, then using that bound to give you globally consistent transactions. It's the paper where physics literally leaks into the system design diagram. Wildest flex in distributed databases 🌍 [link] |
| 13 | 2026-08-30 | Amazon Aurora: Design Considerations for High-Throughput Cloud-Native Relational Databases — Amazon (2017) | Aurora's core idea: stop shipping the whole database log around, ship it once to a smart storage layer instead. It's the paper that explains why "cloud-native database" isn't just marketing — it's a genuinely different architecture from "Postgres, but on a bigger VM" ☁️ [link] |
| 14 | 2026-08-31 | Calvin: Fast Distributed Transactions for Partitioned Database Systems — Yale/Thomson Reuters (2012) | Calvin's bet: decide transaction order deterministically up front, and a lot of distributed transaction pain just disappears. It's the quieter cousin of Spanner, but the deterministic-execution idea shows up all over modern distributed databases now ⚡ [link] |
| 15 | 2026-09-01 | Attention Is All You Need — Vaswani et al. (2017) | Every LLM conversation I have with a PM now traces back to one 2017 paper that killed recurrence and said "attention is enough, actually." Table stakes reading if you want to understand literally anything happening in AI right now, agentic or not 🤖 [link] |
| 16 | 2026-09-02 | ReAct: Synergizing Reasoning and Acting in Language Models — Princeton/Google (2023) | Turns out most "agents" you've used are just ReAct with better branding — think, act, observe, repeat. This paper is the actual origin of the reasoning loop under most agent frameworks today, and it's a genuinely simple idea once you see it written out 🔁 [link] |
| 17 | 2026-09-03 | Chain-of-Thought Prompting Elicits Reasoning in Large Language Models — Wei et al. (2023) | "Just ask it to think step by step" sounds like a meme until you read the paper showing it's the actual unlock for multi-step reasoning in LLMs. Foundation for basically every prompt engineering trick that came after 🧠 [link] |
| 18 | 2026-09-04 | Self-Consistency Improves Chain of Thought Reasoning in Language Models — Wang et al. Pairs perfectly with Chain-of-Thought as the "and also do this" follow-up ✅ [link] |
| 19 | 2026-09-05 | Toolformer: Language Models Can Teach Themselves to Use Tools — Meta (2023) | Before "function calling" was a button in every LLM provider's docs, Toolformer showed models could learn when and how to call APIs on their own. | 20 | 2026-09-06 | Gorilla: Large Language Model Connected with Massive APIs — UC Berkeley (2023) | Toolformer proved models could learn to use tools; Gorilla is what happens when you scale that idea to thousands of real-world APIs. | 21 | 2026-09-07 | Reflexion: Language Agents with Verbal Reinforcement Learning — Noah Shinn et al.
